# Introduction

ChronoCone is a generic planning system designed to solve a wide variety of planning problems. It provides a flexible framework that supports automated planning, human-in-the-loop planning, and collaborative planning across different organizational levels.

The original concept of ChronoCone envisioned capturing metrics as a DAG where some form of function (including, but not ultimately limited to, the example metric python function examples captured in the example directory). Data would flow in via some metric(s) generated by human input or adapters, some calculations would be
performed to create the entries in another metric and so on until the ultimate planning outputs are created. This concept works fine as long as there are simple relationships between the inputs and outputs. However, it starts to break down when two or more variables influence each other. For example, in the retail_plan.md example future orders are influenced by current and future inventory projections but future inventory projections are influenced by the timing and amount of orders, which in turn influences the cost of buying from vendors. These relationships cannot be captured by a DAG and optimal solutions can only be found by search or optimization procedures that are not expected to be common in the target audience for ChronoCone - namely business analysts with a deep understanding of their domain (e.g., retail sales, warehouse operations), but without deep understanding of statistics or optimization algorithms. In order to satisfy both the requirement to work with the mental model of the user and to handle complex multivariable relationships some mechanism must be devised that allows simple feedforward values to be captured when that is possible but also allows information about relationships between variables to be captured when they interact with each other. This document explores one approach to accomplish that end.

One of the defining features of ChronoCone is that it relies heavily on time series. That's motivated by the fact that business operations are required to observe the arrow of time. Orders must be placed before inventory can later become available and not the reverse. Trucks must arrive at warehouses before they can be loaded, labor must be allocated and scheduled at a warehouse before work can be done in the warehouse and so on. In the proposed formulation timeseries continue to be just as important, but the core idea is that the individual entries in a time series are no longer simple values such as int or float, but are instead vector variables that can be assigned values (possibly multiple values) but also have relationships with each other.

Note: The examples in this document are captured in python in order to keep the discussion concrete. However, as with the original metrics concept the metrics may be converted into a Control and Data Flow graph (CDFG) and "executed" using a variety of possible techniques. In ChronoCone V0 it's anticipated that the python functions will simply be executed, however. Also, the exact decorator names and parameters are intended to be illustrative rather than definitive.

Metrics are captured by a set of python functions with decorators. They are intended to be auto-wired (that is, they are simply dropped into the system and the system will automatically organize them properly, flagging any issues).

We are using the example of the retail plan example to illustrate the concept.
The overall flow of the retail plan is:

1. Demand forecasts are generated by an external system (forecasted sales by price point)
2. Vendors make available ordering terms for their products (i.e., this also comes from an outside system). Within some ranges ordering a larger number of units will result in a lower price per unit, but in many cases require a longer lead time.
3. It's imperative that sufficient inventory be available at all time, somewhere in the fulfillment network, to meet the projected demand forecast at whatever price point is selected.
4. The goal of the Retail group is to maximize the net revenue of the business (i.e., top line revenue minus the cost of units bought from the vendors).

The following diagram shows the overall flow.

```
Demand Forecast (by Product, Price)
         ↓
    [Select Price Point for each Product/Week]
         ↓
Selected Price + Anticipated Sales
         ↓
Vendor Availability (by Product, Vendor, MinQty)
         ↓
    [Select Vendor and Quantity considering:]
    [- Price per unit]
    [- Lead time vs. inventory buffer needs]
    [- 20% max buffer constraint]
         ↓
Orders Submitted → Item Cost → Anticipated Vendor Cost
         ↓
Unit Sales → Top Line Revenue → Net Revenue
```

# Input Metrics

The first two metrics are input metrics. They are generated by external systems and are not part of the Retail plan. They are simply consumed by the Retail plan. The Demand Forecast metric is a time series of the forecasted sales by price point. The Vendor Availability metric is a time series of the ordering terms for each product where each price is associated with a range of order quantity and an associated lead time.

## Demand Forecast Metric

The demand forecasting system provides a forecast of the number of units that will be sold for each product at each price point looking out for 6 months. For example, for Product A there might be two forecast time series, one if the product is sold at \$10/unit and the other if the product is sold at \$20/unit. The elements of the time series are the integer number of units that are forecasted to be sold at the corresponding price in that week.

A "forecast metric function" defines the demand forecast metric.

```
# Don't take this too literally, it's actually wrong...just concentrate on the concepts
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Demand Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(("units", int))
@adapter
def demand_forecast(product:str, moment:datetime) -> Value[int]:
    return DemandForecastAdapter.get(product, moment)
```

The @metric decorator declares that this is a metric, with a human readable description and an association with the Retail namespace family. The type=FORECAST parameter indicates that this is a forecast metric (as opposed to an associated metric that represents actuals). For the moment don't worry about the details too much (like, where do the product values come from, how does the calculation engine know what to do with the sales price?), just try to understand the concepts. We'll revisit this down below to iron out the details.

The @timeseries decorator indicates that this is a time series metric with a weekly grain and a length of 26 weeks.

The @categories decorator indicates that this metric has two categories, Product and SalesPrice. The metric can be thought of as a datacube where the dimensions are Product, SalesPrice and time. As we'll see later, in order for two metrics to be combined in a calculation, they must have the same categories, although that will often be accomplished by applying some aggregation or selection operator to one or more of the input metrics.

The name of the metric is always the same as the name of the function. In this case, the metric is named "demand_forecast". Because this is an input metric (meaning that it does not have any other metrics as inputs), there are no metrics named in the parameters. However, each metric may optionally name one or more of its categories as parameters, plus the system defined value "moment", which is the date/time of the entry in a timeseries. Moment is available only for time series metrics.

The @adapter decorator indicates that an adapter is being used in the metric (note: I'm not entirely sure this is necessary, but going with it for the moment - Doug). Adapters, which are only used on ultimate input and ultimate output metrics have special needs, such as authentication to access external systems.
In this case the product and week are brought into the metric in order to pass them to the adapter. The underlying calculation engine will invoke the metric function for each product/sales price/week combination. While a detailed design for adapters is beyone the scope of this document, it should be noted that a naive implementation would result in the adapter making one call to a service for each product/sales price/week combination making the adapter very chatty. It's likely that in many cases the requests will be batched. Thus each call to the adapter should accumulate the data in a context somewhere so that the computation framework can gather together individual data items to be gathered and transform them into a larger request or requests.

The "actuals" for a metric are the actual values that were observed for whatever the metric represents. The actual and forecast entries for a metric must have the same categories and grain. For input metrics the actuals may be gotten from a different system than forecast inputs. In this case the demand forecast system is different from the system that tracks actual sales, so the actuals for the metric are captured by a "actuals" metric function.

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Demand Forecast", type=ACTUALS)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(("product", str))
@adapter
def demand_forecast(product:str, moment:datetime) -> Value[str]:
    return SalesSystemAdapter.getForecast(product, moment)
```

Like forecasts, the execution engine for ChronoCone is responsible for invoking the actuals metric function for the correct time period (e.g., it might gather one more weeks worth of actuals for the demand_forecast metric once the week is over).

Actuals for non-input metrics will be calculated within ChronoCone based on the actuals values of the inputs. We'll revisit this point later.

## Vector Values

The return type of demand_forecast is Value[float], which means that the metric returns an actual datum of the type given (here, an integer representing the number of units sold). However, in some cases we'll have data that is tightly correlated, such as the price and lead time of a vendor order for a given number of units ordered. In order to accomodate those kinds of scenarios, Values (and as we'll see later, Variables) are actually tuples/vectors of values. The individual values within Values aren't just random mixtures of values, instead they will usually have some meaning such as the number of units ordered determining the vendor price and lead time. This is a functional dependency in database terms and we'll borrow the concepts of keys to represent these kinds of relationships, which is captured in the @values decorator.

The general form of the @values decorator is:

```
@values(key=(("name", type)), values=(("name", type), ...))
```

where the key is the value that determines the other values in the tuple. A key may have multiple values, keys are expected to be the primary information in a Value, with the other elements being subordinate to it. Should it happen that there is a multivalued Value in which the individual data elements are independent of each other, they would all be part of the key. If no key value is specified, then it'll be assumed that (what? that the key is the first value? that the key is the entire tuple? - Doug 2/12/2025)

## Multivalue Vector Example

For the sake of example, imagine that the demand_forecast metric were defined to include both the number of units forecasted to be sold and the top live revenue forecast as values in the timeseries. The forecast metric function could be redefined as:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Demand Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(key=("units", int), values=(("revenue", float)))
@adapter
def demand_forecast(product:str, salesprice:float, moment:datetime) -> Value[int, float]:
    forecast_units_sold = DemandForecastAdapter.get(product, moment)

    return forecast_units_sold, forecast_units_sold * salesprice
```

in this case the other category is included in the input parameters, the units are defined as the key (since the key predicts the top line revenue) and the revenue is a dependent value. Both values are returned from the forecast function and a similar modification would need to be applied to the actuals function.

## Simple Feedforward Metrics

In previous example the revenue follows in a simple feed forward manner from the sales price and unit forecast so it would equally possible to define the top line revenue as an independent metric - and that might be preferable given the reporting needs of the organization. If that was so, then we would keep the original definition of demand_forecast and define a new metric for top line revenue, thus:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Top Line Revenue", type=FORECAST)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(("revenue", float))
@adapter
def top_line_revenue(demand_forecast: Value[int],
                     salesprice:float, moment:datetime) -> Value[float]:
    return demand_forecast.units * salesprice
```

The top line revenue is defined on the same time grain with the same categories as its only input metric, so there is no need for aggregation or other operations to get the dimensions to match. The ChronoCone execution engine will essentially do a map operation across the demand_forecast metric and store the top_line_revenue metric values in the corresponding entries in the timeseries for the top_line_revenue metric. Note that each element of a Value vector is available as an attribute of that has the same name as that declared in the @values decorator in the forecast or actuals metrice definition.

### Demand Forecast, Revisited

Consider the demand_forecast metric definition from earlier in this document:

```
# Don't take this too literally, it's actually wrong...just concentrate on the concepts
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Demand Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(("units", int))
@adapter
def demand_forecast(product:str, moment:datetime) -> Value[int]:
    return DemandForecastAdapter.get(product, moment)
```

So far we've been ignoring a couple of basic issues. The demand_forecast can be invoked for each product, but how does ChronoCone know which products are available for sale? Also, how does it know to automagically create a metric with SalesPrice as a category? The answer is that it can't, so in this section we're going to reformulate it to make it work, and introduce a new concept at the same time.

For the calculation engine to associated the right values in the inputs to a metric with the outputs of that metric the categories need to match so that new_metric(categoryA, categoryB) = existing_metric(categoryA, categoryB). What happens if they don't match? There are three cases to consider:

1. The new metric has one or more categories that aren't present in the metric(s) it takes as input(s). In this case data can be "lifted" up to one or more of the category dimensions by supplying a map from the missing category(ies) to the values of the final metric.
2. The new metric is missing one or more metrics that are present in the metric(s) that it takes as input(s). In this case an aggregation operation will need to be performed, the aggregation operation to be applied would be noted for each metric that's an input to the new metric.
3. The metrics are simply disjoint. It's possible that they could be brought into alignment using some combination of input aggregation and output lifts, but it's also possible that the dimensions used through the plan need to be rethought.

In this example we're going to use output lifting to align the Product and SalesPrice categories with the desired dimensions of demand_forecast.

First, a list of products needs to be read in from the Sales system, as follows. For this example we'll assume that there are only actuals for products, but there could be forecasts as well (e.g., for planned products that aren't available yet).

Possible definition for the products metric might be just a list of product names, thus:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Products", type=ACTUALS)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK)
@categories()
@values(("products", List[str]))
@adapter
def products() -> Value[List[str]]:
    # Assume this returns a flat list.
    return SalesSystemAdapter.getProducts()
```

Note that this product definition has no categories associated with it, since it's just a flat list.

However, for the purpose of this example let's assume that each product has a description, in which case the metric would become:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Products", type=ACTUALS)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK)
@categories("Product")
@values(("description", str))
@adapter
def products() -> Annotated[Value[str], Out(aggregate=lift("Product"))]:
    # Assume this returns a hashmap of product names to descriptions.
    return SalesSystemAdapter.getProductsWithDescription()
```

The new element that has been introduced is an output annotation, which in this case promises that the output of from the metric function instead of being a single description value to match the declared values of the metric will instead be a map from something that the function is promising is a Product Value to something that's the declared value(s) of the metric.

Now we can define demand_forecast properly:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Demand Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@categories("Product", "SalesPrice")
@values(("units", int))
@adapter
def demand_forecast(products:Value[str], moment:datetime) -> Annotated[Value[int], Out(aggregate=lift("SalesPrice"))]:
    # Assume that this returns a hashmap of SalesPrice to forecasted units that will be sold for the given product and week.
    return DemandForecastAdapter.getProductSalesForecast(product, moment)
```

Because the only input metric (products, not to be confused with the category Product/product) has categories/dimensions of Product the evaluation engine would normally assume that that was the matching category for the output metric. However, the lift annotation on the output of the metric function tells the evaluation engine that the output is actually a map from SalesPrice to the declared value(s) of the metric and that it should use that to flesh out the SalesPrice category for the demand_forecast metric.

## Handling Interacting Metrics and Metric Values

Up to this point we've been dealing with simple feedforward metrics where the value of one metric can be directly derived from the values of another metric. However, there will be many cases where multiple values will interact with each other, making simple calculations impossible. To handle those cases, we need Variable.

Variable is like Value, except that instead of being assigned an explicit value in metric functions constraints on it's value and/or relationships to other metrics are defined.

For example, let's define the following metrics to determine ordering quantities and times for products:

1. We need to maintain a minimum inventory level at the beginning of each week that is equal to the demand forecast for that week plus a 20% buffer but shouldn't exceed 100% of the forecast demand for the following four weeks.
2. We need to take acount of order amounts vs. price paid and the lead time for orders, which varies by quantity.
3. We want to maximum the difference between the average top line revenue and the average cost of goods sold.

An input metric for this example is taken from a vendor management system and is a cost schedule for purchases by price, minimum quantity and lead time. Generally larger quantities can be bought for lower prices, but will need to allow for longer lead times. There may or may not be multiple vendors for any given product.

The first metric tells us what vendors can be used for a given product. The values are the name, address and phone number of the vendor.

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Vendors For Product", type=ACTUALS)
@categories("Product", "Vendor")
@timeseries(grain=WEEK)
@values(key=("name", str), values=(("address", str), ("phone", str)))
@adapter
def vendors_for_product(products:str, vendor:str) -> Annotated[Value[str, str, str], Out(aggregate=lift("Vendor"))]:
    vendor_info_by_name = {}

    # Assume this returns a flat list of vendor names
    vendor_list = VendorAvailabilityAdapter.getVendors(product)

    for vendor in vendor_list:
        vendor_info = VendorAvailabilityAdapter.getVendorInfo(vendor)
        vendor_info_by_name[vendor] = (vendor_info.address, vendor_info.phone)


    return vendor_info_by_name
```

This is another metric that has actuals but no forecast. For each week we're keeping track of the vendors that we can get each product from, and that may change over time. It's possible that we'd also want to generate a forecast by including changes in the future as vendors are anticipated to be added or removed, but I'm going to treat this as an actuals only metric for this example.

The product_availability metric is used to keep the product pricing schedule by vendor and product and is defined as follows:

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Product Availability", type=ACTUALS)
@categories(("Product",str), ("Vendor",str), ("Unit Order Range", Range[int, int]))
@timeseries(grain=WEEK)
@values(key=("price_per_unit", float),values=(("lead_time_weeks", int)))
@adapter
def product_availability(vendors_for_product:Value[str],
                         product:str,
                         vendor:str) -> Annotated[Value[float, Range[int, int], int],Out(aggregate=lift("Unit Order Range"))]:
    result = {}

    # Assume this is a flat list of elements where each element is an object with attributes price_per_unit, min_units to qualify for that price point and lead_time_in_days plus some other data we don't care about.
    price_list = VendorAvailabilityAdapter.getPriceList(product, vendor)

    # The naming and format returned by the VendorAvailabilityAdapter isn't quite what we want for the adapter,
    # so we need to restructure it.

    # The metric defines a range of number units ordered for each price point, but the data we get back has a list of price points and the minimum number of units you need to order to get that price point.
    sorted_by_min_order = sorted(price_list, key=lambda x: x.min_units)

    lower_unit_range = 1
    for i in range(1, len(sorted_by_min_order)):
        upper_unit_range = sorted_by_min_order[i].min_units - 1
        result[Range(lower_unit_range, upper_unit_range)] = (
            sorted_by_min_order[i-1].price_per_unit,
            sorted_by_min_order[i-1].lead_time_in_days)
        lower_unit_range = sorted_by_min_order[i].min_units

    return result
```

Up to this point we've defined metrics that can be calculated using some business rules and formulas in a simple feedforward manner. Now we're going to start working with orders and inventory availability, which have mutual dependencies in the sense that changing one of them changes all of the others, but with feedback. For example, if we order more units now but with a longer lead time we'd get a better price, but in some weeks we might run out of inventory. To avoid that we need to take into account the effect on the inventory forecast of ordering time and volume, so order timing and volume affects the inventory forecast, but the inventory forecast affects order timing and volume. Teasing out these interacting effects is the domain of optimization algorithms.

To handle that a new concept is being introduced, the Variable. Unlike Values, a variable isn't assigned a value by the metric function, instead it defines the thing that can be changed (i.e., a decision variable), and the constraints that must be satisfied if the solution is to be valid. The ChronoCone system will be responsible for figuring out what the "best" value is for the variable given the constraints.

The order_placement_plan captures when orders are placed, with which vendor and for how many units. Note that this is "plan" rather than a "forecast", because it represents decisions that we're making, rather than a prediction about an outside influence.

Note that there is no attempt to calcuate the value assigned to this element of the timeseries, we're only describing what a valid solution would look like.

One of the issues that we face is that it may be necessary to order from multiple vendors who will be selling at different price points. How should that be represented in the metric? The decision here is to keep track of the average price per unit, which is the price points weighted by the number of units ordered at each price point. From a mechanical point of view this decision is forced on the planner by the need to match the dimensions of the product availability metric (Product, Vendor, Unit Order Range) with the dimensions of the order*placement_plan metric (Product, Vendor). \_Something* must be done to collapse the Unit Order Range dimension, and the average price per unit is likely to be the one most relevant to the business.

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Order Placement Plan", type=FORECAST)
@categories(("Product",str), ("Vendor",str))
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@values(("units_ordered", int),("price_per_unit", float))
def order_placement_plan(product_availability: Annotated[Value[int], In(aggregation=group_by("Unit Order Range"))], # By Product, Vendor and Unit Order Range
                         network_inventory_forecast: Annotated[Variable[int], In(aggregation=group_by("Vendor","Avg Price Per Unit"))], # By Product
                        ) -> Variable[int, float]:
    order_placement: Variable[int] = Variable()

    # Constraints. If this particular order is placed we'll incur the given price but only if the
    # number of units ordered is within the range specified in the product_availability metric.
    order_placement.price_per_unit = product_availability.price_per_unit
    product_availability.unit_order_range[0] <= order_placement.units_ordered <= product_availability.unit_order_range[1]

    # Inventory is not actually available until the lead time has passed, so this is an assertion about what will happen some number of
    # weeks from the moment represented by the current timeseries element.
    network_inventory_forecast.future(timedelta(weeks=product_availability.lead_time_weeks)).units += sum(order_placement.units_ordered)

    return order_placement
```

Next, we create the inventory forecast, which contains feedback from the order_placement_plan. The inventory forecast doesn't care which vendor is being used, or what price is being paid, it just wants to know how many units are available. The mechanics of the mismatch between the the dimensions of the demand_forecast and the network_inventory_forecast again force the planner to make a decision about how to match them up. The solution in this case is to invoke an "aggregation" function that asserts that exactly one of the potential price points in the demand_forecast for any given week will be used, something that's implicitly assumed by the business.

Note: The "must choose one price for a week" feels like a constraint that belongs in the definition of the demand_forecast metric. That would collapse the SalesPrice dimension/category, but that category needs to be available for the order_placement_plan metric. We should think about that. - Doug Thomae 2/14/2026

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Network Level Inventory Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@categories(("Product",str))
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@values(("units", int))
def network_inventory_forecast(demand_forecast: Annotated[Value[int], In(aggregation=choose("Sales Price"))], # Product, SalesPrice
                               ) -> Variable[int]:
    inventory = Variable()

    # Constraints
    inventory >= demand_forecast * 1.2 # Inventory must match the demand forecast plus 20% buffer

    # The inventory should not exceed the total demand forecasted for the next 4 weeks
    sum(demand_forecast.future(Range(timedelta(weeks=0), timedelta(weeks=4))).units)<=inventory
```

Finally, we get down to the bottom line: the net revenue forecast. This metric is calcuated by a purely feedforward, so the metric function returns a Value instead of a Variable. A new decorator, @goal, is used to indicate that this metric is to be maximized.

```
@metric(ns_family="Retail", description="Net Revenue Forecast", type=FORECAST)
@categories(("Product",str))
@timeseries(grain=WEEK, length=26)
@values(("net_revenue", int))
@goal("maximize")
def net_revenue_forecast(demand_forecast: Annotated[Value[int], In(aggregation=choose("Sales Price"))], # Product, SalesPrice
                         order_placement_plan : Annotated[Variable[str, str], In(aggregation=group_by("Vendor"))], # Product, Vendor
                         ) -> Value[int]:
    return (demand_forecast.units * demand_forecast.price_per_unit) - (order_placement_plan.units_ordered * order_placement_plan.price_per_unit)
```

# Why Do Things This Way?

If the calculations in ChronoCone are limited to simple feedforward calculations, then it would be essentially a multi-dimensional spreadsheet where the formulas are implemented as Python functions (in a V0 prototype, as CDFGs in a more developed version). While that might be useful, it would not be able to handle planning that involved multiple interacting variables. For that, optimization techniques are required, and it's my suspicion that most of the real world planning problems that businesses have are likely to be solvable with linear programming, which is a well established and well understood technique that produces guaranteed optimal solutions. (Note: the freely available LP solvers are slower or more limited than the commercial ones, but I expect they are good enough for a prototype. In a full-up product, it would probably be necessary to license a commercial solver.).

In the case of this example it might appear that the problem is non-linear due to terms like (# units)(unit cost or price), but in fact it isn't _because the number of units isn't a continuous variable, but rather there's a set of discrete values that it can take on_ in both the demand forecast and the vendor price schedule. I believe that these are likely to be common situations. In cases where that's not true that are non-linear solution techniques that can be used, but they are normally not guaranteed to find the optimal solution.

The language of optimization problems (in general, not just for linear problems) that that of constraints and objective functions. The operations chosen in this example were made because I think that they will make it possible to deduce the set of variables required to solve the problem and assemble them into the necessary constraints and objective functions.

# A Note On Nomenclature

In this example I've used the theoretical Value and Variable types to distinguish straight forward feedforward calculations that would just be a call given that their inputs are available vs cases where variables would need to be assembled and passed to a solver in order to find a result. However, having them be different types isn't a necessity since we can distinguish the two cases by their usage. A simple calculation will assign a value to the output, but a metric that translates into a variable won't have a value assigned, it will just have constraints associated with it. It seems likely that a business analyst creating functions that define a plan will naturally tend to enter a formula when that's possible (similar to what they'd do in Excel), and will only start to use definitions without a directly computed value when they don't have a way to do that because of multiple interacting variables. Thus, their natural instincts and preferences will tend to guide them in the direction of the most appropriate approach.

When a function is defined with constraints but no value there's a natural translation into the optimization problem space. When both constraints and values are assigned there's also a natural interpretation - the "constraints" are just pre or post conditions that can be evaluated and when not satisfied will be the source of information that can be used to alert the user when a plan needs manual intervention (there will be similar cases where there is not solution possible given the constraints on the variables). Given that, I think that it makes sense not to distinguish between Value and Variable types in metric functions, but just call them Item or Datum or something and let their treatment be handled by what the user does with them. This is analogous to choosing a dynmaically typed languge over a statically typed language. Given that most business analysts will likely be more comfortable with the former, I think that it would be a better choice.

# Python Types And The Planning DSL

The choices in this example are leading in the direction of a DSL for metrics, which is obviously quite incomplete at the moment. I think that it would be possible to use Python types and operator overloading to capture the necessary information to build the optimization problem when that's necessary, but I won't know for sure until we've tried building out some examples.
